# What the Remilink Founder Must Think Through and Provide to the Development Team

No developer can invent this for you. The team can build anything — but they cannot decide *what your company is and who you sell to*.

---

## 1. Who Are Your Buyers?

Describe 2–3 most typical client types. For each:

- What is their title? (VP Operations, CTO, CEO of a mid-sized company, Head of Digital?)
- What size is the company? (revenue, headcount)
- What industry are they in?
- What triggered the search for an AI partner? (competitors adopted AI, board pressure, specific operational pain, failed attempt to build in-house)
- What are they afraid of? (spending budget on a prototype that never reaches production; picking the wrong partner; getting slide decks instead of working systems; internal team resistance)
- What would convince them to start a conversation?
- Where do they come from? (Google, referral, LinkedIn, conference, article)

Mostly LinkedIn.

- What does their vendor selection process look like? (single decision-maker or committee? how many steps? who else evaluates?)

This is not an abstract exercise. Every page on the site will speak to these people. If you can't name them, the site speaks to no one.

---

## 2. What Is the One Action a Visitor Should Take?

Pick one:

- Fill out a form and get a response from you
- Book a discovery call via calendar
- Email you directly
- Download a resource (white paper, case study PDF)

Everything else is secondary. The entire site funnels toward this one action. If you say "all equally important," the site will be weak at all of them.

Also decide:
- What happens after someone submits an inquiry? (Auto-reply? Personal email within 24 hours? Phone call?)
- Who handles incoming leads and how fast?
- Is there a lead qualification step before the call?

---

## 3. Your Services — Structured and Honest

For each service line, provide:

- **What you actually do** — not abstractions like "AI transformation," but real actions (run a data audit, architect a RAG pipeline, build an MLOps platform, train the internal team)
- **What the client gets** — specific deliverables (roadmap, working prototype, production system, trained internal team)
- **How long it typically takes** — typical timelines (90-day sprint, 6-month engagement)
- **Who it's for** — what type of company, at what stage of AI maturity
- **How it's different** from what others offer — in plain language
- **What's included and what's not** — clear boundaries to avoid inflated expectations

Write as if you're explaining to a potential client in a meeting, not in marketing language. The team will turn it into good copy — but they need the raw truth.

---

## 4. Pricing and Transparency

Decide clearly:

- Do you show prices or price ranges on the site? (Yes / No / "Projects starting from $X")
- If no — what do you show instead? ("Let's discuss on a call" / "Depends on scope")
- What is your pricing model? (fixed project fee, time & materials, retainer, value-based?)
- Is there a low-barrier "entry product"? (paid audit, strategy session, fixed-price pilot?)

For B2B AI consulting, hiding prices is the norm — but having an "entry product" with a clear price dramatically lowers the barrier to first contact.

---

## 5. Proof and Social Evidence

Gather real materials:

- **Case studies** — for each: what was the problem, what did you do, what was the measurable result. Even with anonymous clients (NDA), specificity in describing the challenge and outcome builds trust.
- **Specific metrics** — "68% faster underwriting," "30% patient throughput improvement," "98% defect detection accuracy" — numbers already on the current site. How real are they? Are there more?
- **How many projects** have you actually completed? The current site says "100+ AI initiatives delivered" — is that an accurate figure?
- **"Top 0.001% Engineering excellence worldwide"** — what's behind this? What ranking, what platform, what criteria? If this cannot be backed by a concrete source, it's better to remove or rephrase.
- **"4.9/5 Client trust rating"** — where does this number come from? Clutch? Google Reviews? Internal survey? The source must be named, or the metric should not be used.
- **Are there clients willing to provide a quote or testimonial?** Even an anonymous quote with title and industry ("VP Operations, manufacturing company") is more valuable than bare numbers.
- **Any press mentions, speaking engagements, publications, partnerships?**

If proof is thin — say so honestly. The team can build trust around a small number of strong cases. Around zero — they cannot.

---

## 6. Founder and Team

### Founder

The founder is the face of the company. In B2B AI consulting, where clients are buying expertise and trust, the founder's identity is critically important.

What to prepare:

- Professional but natural photo (not a corporate headshot in a suit, but not a selfie either)
- Additional photo: with the team, at an event, in a working environment
- Brief story — *why* you started Remilink (in human terms, not an autobiography)
- Key facts: education, prior experience (companies, roles), how many years in AI/ML
- What sets you apart from hundreds of other "AI consultants" — specific experience, specialization, approach
- Your role in projects — are you personally involved in delivery, or only in sales?

How this will be used on the site:
- **On the homepage** — briefly: photo + one strong line + link to /about/
- **On the About page** — expanded: story, expertise, photos
- **On the blog** — as the author of articles, if you write them yourself

### Team

- How many people on the team? Full-time or contract? Or a mix?
- Are there key people worth featuring? (with photo and brief bio)
- Or is Remilink primarily the founder + a network of vetted specialists? If so — that's perfectly fine, but the site should reflect this honestly rather than creating the illusion of a large company.

## 7. Visual Identity

Provide everything you have:

- Logo (SVG + PNG) — the current gradient "REMILINK" wordmark?
- Brand colors — current orange (#ff7a00)? Is there a full palette?
- Fonts — current Satoshi? Is there a web license?
- Brandbook — the project has brandbook_chatgpt_guide.docx and brandbook_chatgpt_visuals.docx files. Are these still current?
- Photos: founder, team, work environments
- Any other visual assets (icons, illustrations, presentations)?

If the brandbook is outdated or incomplete — say so directly. The team can work with what exists, but they shouldn't have to guess what's current.

---

## 8. Competitors and Inspirations

List:

- **3–5 companies** you get compared to or that compete for the same clients. For each: what they do well, what they get wrong, how you're different.
- **3–5 websites** (any industry) whose style, tone, or structure you admire. Explain *why*. (antigravity.google is already chosen as the visual reference — are there others, especially for tone and content?)
- **Anything you explicitly don't want to look like.** "Don't make us look like Accenture" or "I don't want to look like a typical AI startup page with blue gradients" is just as valuable as positive direction.

---

## 9. Content You Already Have

Take inventory:

- **Current site** — what's worth keeping, what's outdated, what doesn't reflect reality?
- **Blog** — 4 articles already exist. Are they still current? Does anything need updating?
- **Case studies** — 6 cases on the current site. For each: is this a real project? Are the metrics accurate? Can the description be expanded (challenge / approach / result)?
- **Presentations, proposal documents, white papers** — any materials you can pull language from?
- **LinkedIn or other social media content** that performed well?

The team needs to know what raw material exists. Rewriting existing content is 3x faster than writing from scratch.

---

## 10. Industries and Specialization

Answer directly:

- The current site lists 8 industries (Financial Services, Healthcare, Manufacturing, Retail, Professional Services, Oil & Gas, Logistics, EdTech). **Have you actually worked in all of them?**
- The PRD proposes focusing on 3: Manufacturing, E-commerce, EdTech. Is that the right call? Or is there an industry you can't drop?
- Is there an industry where you have the most case studies and depth?
- Is there an industry you're deliberately targeting (even if case studies are thin so far)?

Narrowing focus to 3 industries makes Remilink look like a specialist, not a generalist. But this must be your decision, based on real experience.

---

## 11. Operations and Lead Handling

The site will generate leads. Be ready to answer:

- Where should form submissions go? (The current site uses formsubmit.co — is that sufficient?)
- Do you need CRM integration (HubSpot, Pipedrive, other)?
- Who responds to inquiries and within what timeframe? (The site currently says "We'll reach out soon" — can you be more specific?)
- Do you need calendar integration (Calendly, Cal.com) for discovery calls?
- Do you want automated responses or manual follow-up?
- What does your sales pipeline look like after first contact?

---

## 12. Analytics and Measurement

Current site — do you have analytics?

- Google Analytics / Plausible / PostHog — is anything installed?
- Do you know how many visitors per month? Where they come from? How many inquiries?
- How will you know the new site performs better than the old one?

If there's no analytics — that's fine, but it needs to be set up at launch. Without measurement, every content decision is a guess.

---

## 13. What "Done" Looks Like to You

Write in your own words:

- When a VP-level buyer lands on the new site, what should they *feel* in the first 5 seconds?
- What one sentence would you want a potential client to say to a colleague after visiting the site?
- What is the biggest problem with the current site that this redesign must fix?
- What would personally make you proud of the new site?
- What would make you feel it was a waste of time?

This is not fluff. This is the acceptance test the team cannot write for you.

---

## How to Deliver This

No polished document needed. A messy Google Doc with honest answers is worth more than a formatted brief with vague statements. For each item above:

- If you know the answer — write it down
- If you're unsure — write "I need to think about this" — that's a valid answer
- If it doesn't apply — say why

The development team can structure, polish, and design around your raw input. What they cannot do is substitute their judgment for yours when it comes to *your company, your clients, and your business*.
